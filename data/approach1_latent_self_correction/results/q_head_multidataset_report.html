From a single ChartQA study to three datasets and a unified head that scores an answer before and after it is generated.

1. Starting point: the original ChartQA study

This report builds on an earlier study that asked a narrow question: before building anything that corrects a vision-language model, can we tell from the model's own internal state when it is about to be wrong? This section summarizes that study and its numbers. Everything after it is new work.

What the study did

  • Data and model. ChartQA, Qwen3-VL-2B, 2,500 test questions. The model answered 75.84% of them correctly, which leaves about 24% wrong answers to detect.
  • Features. The model was run once per question and two last-layer hidden states were cached: the state at the last prompt token (before any answer, the pre-generation state) and the state at the last token after the model's own answer was appended (the candidate state). A third state, with the reference answer appended, was cached for training pairs. The language model was never updated.
  • Two heads. A pre-generation head reads the first state and judges how likely the question is to be answered correctly. A verifier head reads the second state and judges the specific answer. Both are small networks trained on a correct or wrong label.
  • Training recipe. Two-layer MLP (layer norm, 512 units, GELU, 10% dropout) trained with AdamW at learning rate 0.001, weight decay 0.0001, batch size 512, 20 epochs. Loss: binary cross-entropy plus 0.25 times a batch ranking loss, plus for the verifier 0.25 times a paired loss that scores the reference answer above a wrong generated one. A single temperature fitted on validation calibrated the probabilities. The test split was used once.
  • Sweeps. 72 fits (36 per head): architecture (linear, 256-unit MLP, 512-unit MLP), learning rate (3e-4, 1e-3), ranking weight (0, 0.25) and three seeds, selected on validation error AUPRC. A second sweep added early stopping and tried five ranking weights for the verifier, over three seeds.

What the study found

  • The model's own confidence is nearly useless as a gate. The geometric mean of the answer token probabilities was exactly 1.0 for 1,921 of 2,500 test answers (76.8%), including many wrong ones. Its AUROC was 0.750, and keeping its most confident half of answers only raised accuracy from 75.8% to 92.4%.
  • A small head on frozen states does much better. The best learned scorers reached AUROC 0.89 and error AUPRC about 0.74, cut the Brier score from 0.178 to about 0.11 and ECE from 0.172 to between 0.03 and 0.04, and lifted accuracy on the most trusted half to about 97%.
  • Which head wins depends on the metric. The linear pre-generation probe had the highest AUROC (0.895), which says expected difficulty is close to linearly readable from the question state alone. The early-stopped verifier had the highest error AUPRC (0.738) and is the one that judges a specific answer, so it is the relevant model for a correction decision.
  • The ranking objectives did not help. Plain cross-entropy won both heads in the main sweep. The weak ranking weight (0.01) that won the early-stop sweep beat plain cross-entropy by a small margin that the study itself flagged as needing out-of-distribution validation.

What it left open

The study ended with its own list of next steps: compare features from several layers, test the answer-induced change as an explicit feature (prompt state, answer state, and their difference), train against harder wrong answers, measure seed stability by selecting on the mean over seeds, and validate out of distribution. This report takes up three of those. It checks out of distribution on three more datasets. It selects hyperparameters on the mean of three seeds throughout. And it tests a combined prompt and answer input, by concatenation rather than by difference. Layer choice, the difference feature, and harder negatives remain open.

2. This report in brief

  • The ChartQA result transfers. Across three datasets with different input types (photographs with text, scanned pages and science diagrams), the unified head raises correctness AUROC over geometric-mean token confidence by 0.13 to 0.27. Token confidence is saturated everywhere: it equals 1.0 on 79% to 90% of test answers.
  • One head can do both jobs. The unified head reads the prompt latent and the answer latent together, and with the answer half zeroed it scores a question before any answer exists. Its joint mode matches or beats the separate verifier head on every dataset: the paired bootstrap interval excludes zero in its favor on DocVQA and ScienceQA, includes zero on TextVQA.
  • It works as a gate. Flagging the 20% of answers with the lowest predicted correctness captures 59% to 79% of all errors, against 44% to 53% for token confidence. Keeping only the most trusted half of answers lifts accuracy to 97% to 100%.
  • What the head learns differs by dataset. On TextVQA it mostly catches broken or malformed answers, which only the answer-side state can see. On DocVQA it separates hard layouts and handwriting from easy forms. On ScienceQA it mostly learns which topics are hard, so the question alone is almost enough.
  • The pre-generation loss term costs almost nothing, and the pairwise loss hurts. The pre-generation term lifts that mode on every dataset and moves the joint mode by less than 0.006 AUROC (validation). The pairwise reference-answer loss lowered validation AUROC on every dataset and is off in every winner.
  • What this does not show yet. These heads detect likely errors. They do not fix them. Whether a latent correction step helps on the flagged answers is the next experiment.

4. What was added

  • Three more datasets, chosen for different inputs. TextVQA, DocVQA, and ScienceQA, each with its own correctness rule, grouped splits so no image or document is shared across train, validation, and test, and an offline-tested scoring module (Section 5).
  • A unified head. One network that reads the prompt state and the answer state together, with a loss that also keeps it useful before an answer exists (Section 6).
  • Automation. One command per dataset submits the whole chain on the ICE cluster: build splits, baseline generation, feature extraction, both sweeps, and the analysis. The model weights are fetched once up front, caches live in scratch because the home quota is 30 GB, and GPU jobs request a flexible constraint (H200, H100, L40S, A100, or A40) because the account has no H200 partition. Every job here ran on an L40S.
  • Analysis tooling. A script that reloads each winning head and computes the curves, reliability bins, category tables, case studies, an answer-length baseline, and paired bootstrap intervals used below, and a builder that turns those files into this report.

A note on the splits. Base accuracy is not the same in train and test for every dataset. DocVQA is 93.2% on its training sample against 87.0% on test. The model may have seen some of these public training sets during its own training, which we did not check, and the train sample and the carved validation split also differ in difficulty. The heads are therefore tested under a shift in base rate, and the validation split, which is drawn like the test split, is what sets the calibration temperature.

6. The unified head

Each latent is layer-normalized separately so the two are on a common scale, then concatenated with a one-bit flag that says whether an answer is present. A linear layer or small MLP maps the result to a single correctness logit. With the answer half set to zeros and the flag at 0 the same weights score a question before generation. This lets the gate run first and the answer-conditioned score be computed only when a correction might follow.

Loss

L = BCE(f(prompt, answer), y)                       joint mode
  + w_pre  * BCE(f(prompt, none), y)                pre-generation mode, same weights
  + w_rank * ranking(f(prompt, answer), y)          correct above wrong, off by default
  + w_pair * softplus(f(p, a) - f(p, reference))    wrong rows only, off in all winners

Both modes predict the same label, so the targets never conflict. The risk is a shortcut: the answer latent is the stronger signal, and a head trained only on joint inputs can lean on it and neglect the prompt half, which would make the pre-generation mode poor. The second term is there to prevent that. Both modes are computed on every batch, with no random dropping of the answer half, so the two terms keep a fixed ratio. Both cross-entropy terms are proper scoring rules, so calibration comes from the loss, and a per-mode temperature fitted on validation corrects what is left.

Protocol

Each configuration is trained on three seeds with early stopping on validation. The grid covers architecture (linear, 256-unit MLP, 512-unit MLP), learning rate (3e-4, 1e-3), pre-generation weight (0, 0.5, 1) and pair weight (0, 0.25): 36 configurations and 108 trainings per dataset. The winner is the configuration with the best mean validation score over seeds, using the average of both modes' error AUPRC with AUROC as a tiebreak. The test split is scored once, for the winner only. The separate heads from the earlier sweep are included for comparison, trained on exactly the same cached features. Learned heads are compared with plain token confidence and, in Section 7.6, with a trivial answer-length rule.

Reading the numbers. Token confidence reaches AUROC 0.726 and is exactly 1.0 on 79% of answers. The separate pre-generation head reaches 0.816 and the separate verifier 0.868. The unified head reaches 0.812 in pre-generation mode and 0.867 in joint mode, a difference of +0.055 from seeing the answer. Against the separate heads the unified joint mode is -0.001 (95% interval -0.011 to +0.011), and the pre-generation mode -0.003 (-0.007 to +0.001). A rule that only looks at answer length reaches AUROC 0.612, with correct answers averaging 8 characters and wrong ones 14, so some of the answer-side signal is a length effect and the learned heads add far more than this rule.

What the head is doing. The answer side matters most here: the separate verifier is well ahead of the separate pre-generation head, and the unified head shows the same gap between its two modes. The caught errors below explain why. Many are not subtle perception mistakes but degenerate outputs: a string repeated until the token limit, a run-on list where a phrase was asked for, a paragraph of explanation. Token confidence stays high on them (0.88 to 0.99 on the examples below), because each repeated token is individually likely, while the answer-side state marks them as broken. A question-only score cannot see these failures, which is why seeing the answer helps so much on this dataset.

What it misses. The missed errors are the opposite kind: fluent, plausible short answers that are wrong or disputed. A band name spelled differently from the annotation, a guess of a region where annotators wrote a country, a yes or no question annotated in an unusual way. Nothing in the model's state marks these as shaky, and some may be label noise rather than model error.

False alarms. The head also penalizes unusual answer formats and question types that are usually wrong, even when the answer is correct: digits written with spaces, and watch-time questions where the model often fails. One false alarm is a labeling quirk rather than a model success: the answer is a pair of braces, and it is labeled correct because official-style answer normalization strips punctuation, so three annotators' punctuation-only answers all normalize to the same empty string as the model's. This affects 6 of 2,500 test answers and does not change any conclusion. On this dataset the single unified head gives up nothing measurable in joint mode, but it is a little less well calibrated than the best separate verifier.

What the head is doing. This is the hardest of the datasets to predict: the AUROC values are the lowest of the new datasets, and the unified joint mode is the clearest win over the separate verifier. The caught errors are verbose answers that run past the requested span, handwriting reads, and list-position questions that need careful reasoning over a table.

The label rule is part of the story. Under the strict similarity rule, many of the highest-scored 'errors' are near misses: the model returns the right span with extra words, as in a title with a date range added or a number followed by a unit word. These answers are fluent and confident, and they look just like correct ones to every scorer. They set a ceiling on how well any correctness head can do under this rule, and they are a reason to try a softer label.

False alarms. The head also doubts answers that the rule accepts but that differ from the reference, such as a surname spelled with a different vowel, which is arguably the head being right and the label being lenient. Handwritten questions are where the head is most overconfident, as the category chart in Section 7.5 shows.

Distribution shift. Base accuracy is noticeably higher on the training sample than on the held-out documents, so the head is tested on harder data than it was trained on. Its calibration holds because the temperature is fitted on a validation split drawn like the test split.

Reading the numbers. Token confidence reaches AUROC 0.654 and is exactly 1.0 on 90% of answers. The separate pre-generation head reaches 0.918 and the separate verifier 0.879. The unified head reaches 0.923 in pre-generation mode and 0.925 in joint mode, a difference of +0.003 from seeing the answer. Against the separate heads the unified joint mode is +0.047 (95% interval +0.034 to +0.061), and the pre-generation mode +0.005 (+0.001 to +0.009). A rule that only looks at answer length reaches AUROC 0.526, with correct answers averaging 1 characters and wrong ones 3, so almost none of the answer-side signal is a length effect and the learned heads add far more than this rule. By category, accuracy runs from 81% for natural science (n=1209) to 95% for social science (n=764); the head predicts 83% and 94% respectively.

What the head is doing. This is the strongest result and the most revealing one. The pre-generation mode alone nearly matches the joint mode, and the separate verifier is the weakest learned scorer, below the separate pre-generation head. The answer is a single letter, so the answer-side state has very little to add beyond the question and image, and the verifier's last-token state is a noisier input than the prompt state.

It has learned topics. The caught errors and the false alarms are both dominated by genetics-cross questions (ratios and probabilities from Punnett squares), which the model often gets wrong, so the head flags that whole family, including the instances the model happens to get right. The confident-correct examples are map questions such as which continent or state is highlighted. So much of the gate is a topic detector.

What it misses. The missed errors are confident factual mistakes: a state capital given for the wrong state, a mislabelled animal class, a misread passage. Token confidence is 1.00 on every one of them. Factual recall errors leave no trace in the model's state that this head can find.

Why this needs care. A topic-level gate can look excellent because topics are easy to separate, and it would simply route every genetics question to correction. Whether that is useful depends on whether correction can fix genetics questions, which is the open experiment.

8. What the results mean

1. The signal is real and it transfers

The learned heads beat token confidence on every dataset, and they do so on photographs with text, scanned pages and science diagrams. The model's hidden state at the end of the prompt already encodes much of how hard the question is for it, and the state after the answer encodes more about whether that specific answer is right. The ChartQA finding was not a property of charts.

2. What the answer latent adds depends on how much the answer says

The gap between the unified head's joint mode and its pre-generation mode is TextVQA +0.055, DocVQA +0.043, ScienceQA +0.003 AUROC. Where the answer is free text, as in TextVQA and DocVQA, the answer-side state carries real extra evidence: it reflects degenerate output, run-on text, and misread strings that no question-only score can foresee. In ScienceQA the answer is a single letter among a few options, so the answer-side state has almost nothing to add and the question alone nearly saturates the signal. The benefit of an answer-conditioned verifier scales with how much information the answer itself carries.

3. A pre-generation score is already worth having

In pre-generation mode the head never sees the answer, yet it captures most of the benefit on two of the datasets. Because it runs before generation, it can decide whether to spend extra compute at all. The case studies show what that score is: mostly a model of question difficulty and topic, not a check on the answer.

4. The head learns the model's failure modes, including ones that are not visual

The caught errors on TextVQA are largely format failures (repetition loops, explanations where a short answer was asked for). The missed errors on DocVQA are largely near misses where the answer carries extra words. The ScienceQA head leans on topic. These are properties of the model plus the labeling rule, and a correction step built on this gate will inherit them. A gate that fires on a repetition loop needs a different fix than one that fires on a misread digit.

5. The unified head simplifies without losing accuracy

Matching or beating the separate heads with one model is a practical result: one set of weights, one cached input pair, and a choice of mode at inference. The pre-generation loss term lets the head keep a usable pre-answer score without hurting the answer-conditioned one. In pre-generation mode it is within 0.006 AUROC of the dedicated head on every dataset.

6. The pairwise reference loss does not help

Adding the paired loss reduced mean validation AUROC on every dataset (Section 7.6), by a large margin on DocVQA. A plausible reason, not tested here, is that the reference-answer state differs from a generated-answer state in ways unrelated to correctness, so the head can improve the pair loss by learning that difference instead. The same pattern held for the ranking loss in the original study. Plain cross-entropy is the right default.

7. Calibration is good on average and uneven by category

After temperature scaling the expected calibration error is small on every dataset. The category breakdown shows where it is not: on DocVQA handwritten questions accuracy is well below the head's mean prediction, so the head is overconfident exactly where the model is weakest. A gate built on this score would under-trigger there.

8. Practical meaning for a correction gate

If a second pass costs one extra generation, flagging 20% of answers costs 20% more compute and reaches the capture rates in Section 7.4. Precision is the other side of that trade. With the joint score, between 38% and 57% of the flagged answers are actually wrong, against base error rates of 13% to 19%. That is a 3.0 to 4.0 times enrichment, but it also means the rest of the flagged set is answers that were already right. A correction step therefore has to be safe on correct answers, a requirement that comes straight from the self-correction literature, and these precision numbers say how often it will matter.

9. The label rule shapes the ceiling

Several of the highest-scored errors are not visual mistakes at all. They are right answers that the labeling rule rejects, such as extra words around a correct span. No scorer can separate those from correct answers, so part of the remaining error mass is irreducible under the current rule. A softer correctness label, or a second label for "right but formatted differently", would change the numbers and may be the better target for a correction gate.

9. Limits of these results

  • Detection, not correction. Nothing here shows that a flagged answer can be fixed. A wrong answer the model cannot fix is still a correct detection.
  • One model size. Only Qwen3-VL-2B was run. The 4B model is the natural check.
  • Carved test splits. TextVQA and DocVQA test sets come from the labeled validation split because the official test answers are hidden. They are grouped by image or document, but they are not the official benchmark test sets.
  • Possible overlap with the model's own training data. These are public benchmarks and the model may have seen their training splits. We did not test for this. It affects base accuracy more than it affects whether a head can detect errors, but it is a reason to read base rates with care.
  • Training subsets. TextVQA and DocVQA heads train on 8,000 sampled questions rather than the full train splits.
  • Single separate-head runs. The separate heads have no seed spread. The paired bootstrap accounts for test-set sampling but not for training-seed variation of those heads, so small differences should not be over-read.
  • Threshold choices in the label. The correct label depends on cutoffs (two annotators for TextVQA, ANLS 0.9 for DocVQA). Other cutoffs would shift the base accuracy and the numbers somewhat.
  • Answer normalization quirks. TextVQA scoring follows the official normalization, which strips punctuation and articles. A few answers (6 of 2,500 test answers, 0.2%) match on empty strings after normalization and are labeled correct when a human would not call them so.
  • Error AUPRC is noisy. With 13 to 19% errors on 2,000 to 2,500 questions, error AUPRC moves by a few thousandths between seeds, so differences of that size are not evidence.
  • Question difficulty versus answer quality. A pre-generation score can learn properties of the question set that predict errors without tracking visual evidence, and the ScienceQA case studies suggest it partly does. The planned counterfactual control (corrupting the visual evidence and checking that the score moves) has not been run.
  • Generation settings differ from the original study. This report caps answers at 64 new tokens and uses a short-answer instruction, the original study allowed 512. Degenerate repetition loops are cut off at the cap, which may inflate the number of TextVQA errors relative to an uncapped run.

10. Next steps

  1. Build the correction step and test it with the gate. A first candidate is test-time latent steering that nudges the hidden state toward a higher correctness score and re-answers, compared with a random nudge of the same size and evaluated only on information available at inference.
  2. Strengthen the head cheaply. Cache several layers instead of the last one, add pooled image-token features, add the prompt-to-answer difference feature the original study suggested, and add the logged token statistics as extra inputs.
  3. Change the target to "would a correction help". Once a correction exists, train the gate on cases where it improved the answer, not just on whether the answer was wrong.
  4. Revisit the label. Try softer correctness for span-style answers and report both.
  5. Run the counterfactual and 4B checks listed above.
