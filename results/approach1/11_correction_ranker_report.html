Approach 1, report 11: the learned correction step

Correcting answers by producing better candidates and choosing among them with a model trained for that choice. Two new candidate sources (visual contrastive decoding and zoomed crops), a within-question candidate ranker, best-of-k from 5 up to 16 samples, where the fixes come from, ablations, compute, and case studies. Qwen3-VL-2B, frozen. Full test splits, 9,517 questions; every choice made on validation (6,420 questions) and test read once.

Builds on report 08 (prompted retry, best-of-five and a deeper pass on the full test split) and report 10 (candidate search and within-question measurements).

2. Where this fits

Approach 1 has two halves. Detection asks whether an answer is likely wrong; reports 01 to 06 showed that a small head on the frozen model's hidden states does this far better than the model's own token probability (pooled AUROC about 0.88 against 0.72). The right answer is often in the pool (90.8% at T = 1.3, k = 10), but the correctness head is a weak judge within one question: its pooled AUROC of 0.88 drops to 0.65–0.70 when only answers to the same question are compared, because across questions most of its signal is question difficulty, which is constant inside one question. So this report attacks both halves of selection: better candidates, and a judge trained for the within-question decision.

3. The idea

For a question, collect candidate answers from several sources, encode each distinct answer the same way (the full image and the original question, followed by the answer), and let a ranker pick one. If the greedy answer is the best candidate the ranker keeps it, so the step can only change an answer when another candidate scores higher. Three design rules:

  • Candidates should differ in evidence, not only in noise. Report 10 showed that resampling at higher temperature mostly reproduces the same answer or adds confident wrong ones. The two new sources change what the model attends to: a contrastive comparison against a noised image, and zoomed views.
  • Every candidate is judged in the same context. A zoomed-crop answer is scored on the full image with the original question, exactly like a sampled one, so the ranker compares like with like. The question embedding is the same cached tensor for every candidate, as in reports 05 to 10.
  • The judge is trained for the decision it makes. The ranker's loss compares the candidates of one question with each other, so question difficulty cannot help it; only properties of the answer can.

4. Candidate sources

4.1 Greedy and sampled answers

The greedy answer is the model's normal output. Sampled answers come from the pools of report 10: up to ten per question at T = 1.0 and T = 1.3, top-p 0.95, extended here to 16 per question for the best-of-11+ comparison with a different seed, so samples 11 onwards are independent draws from the same distribution. Pool k is always the greedy answer plus the first k samples.

4.2 Visual contrastive decoding

Some wrong answers come from language priors: the model answers what is typical for the question, not what the image shows. Contrastive decoding removes that part. At every generated token it compares the next-token distribution under the real image with the distribution under a heavily noised copy of the same image (Gaussian noise mixed in with weight αnoise = 0.15, the corruption used in report 06), and decodes greedily from

score(y) = (1 + α) · log p(y | image, question, prefix) − α · log p(y | noised image, question, prefix)

restricted to tokens the real image finds plausible, p(y | image) ≥ 0.1 · max p. A token the model would produce even without seeing the image gets its score pulled down; a token supported by the image keeps it. It is run at α = 0.5 and α = 1.0 and adds up to two candidates. It needs a second forward pass per token, on the noised image.

4.3 Zoomed crops

Qwen3-VL already sees every image at full resolution (the processor does not downscale these images), so a higher-resolution pass adds nothing. What can help is giving small regions more visual tokens: the question is asked on four overlapping crops, each 60% of the width and height, at the four corners, upscaled 2× (longest side capped at 2048 px). A crop may not contain the evidence, and then its answer is often wrong; that is why these candidates go to a ranker instead of replacing the answer. It adds up to four candidates.

4.4 Encoding every candidate the same way

Each distinct answer string is encoded once with a teacher-forced pass over the full image, the original prompt, and the answer. That gives its answer state (the last-layer state at the last token, as the heads use), its teacher-forced answer probability (geometric mean of the answer tokens' probabilities under the clean prompt, the same number for an answer whatever produced it), and the unified head and linear probe scores computed with the cached question embedding.

5. The ranker

For each candidate the ranker sees three things, and outputs one score; the candidate with the highest score is the answer.

Network. Both states are layer-normalized and concatenated with the nine scalar features, then one hidden layer of 512 units (GELU, dropout 0.2) gives the score. Loss. For a question with candidates c1…cn, scores si and the set R of right candidates,

L = log Σ_i exp(s_i) − log Σ_(i in R) exp(s_i)   (+ β · BCE(s_i, right_i), β in {0, 0.3})

a softmax over the question's candidates with every right one as a target. It is zero for questions whose candidates are all right or all wrong, so only questions where the choice matters train the ranker, and since every term compares candidates of the same question, question difficulty cancels out.

Training data. All 50,517 training questions: the greedy answer and eight samples at T = 1.0 for every question, plus the six correction candidates for a spread subset of 3,200 (400 per shard). Each epoch redraws every question's set (k from {3, 5, 8}, with the corrections 70% of the time when available) so the vote features cover the pool sizes used at test time. Selection. Three variants (states and scalars, scalars only, states only) × 2 learning rates × 2 BCE weights, three seeds each, eight epochs, best epoch by validation accuracy. The reported ranker averages the scores of its three seeds. The candidate-set configuration (temperature, k, which corrections) was then chosen on validation: T1_k5_all, validation gain +1.21 points. Test was read once for it.

Answer: going past five samples does not pay. The oracle keeps rising with k, because each extra sample sometimes contains the right answer, but the ranker's accuracy is flat from k = 3 to k = 16 and the head's falls. Extra samples are mostly repeats of answers already in the pool or confident alternatives that are wrong; each one costs a generation and an encoding pass. The candidates that do help are the ones that change the evidence (Section 8): zoom and contrastive decoding with no samples at all already reach 83.76%. If more compute is available it is better spent on more evidence-changing candidates (finer crop grids, crops chosen by attention) or a stronger judge than on more samples.

9. Why it works: a better judge within a question

Report 10 introduced two measurements of the selection decision itself: grouped AUROC (how often a right candidate scores above a wrong candidate of the same question) and mixed-pool accuracy (how often the top candidate is right, on questions that have both). Here they compare the ranker with the head and with the vote share, on identical candidate sets.

On every candidate set the ranker is the better within-question judge, in grouped AUROC and, more to the point, in how often its top choice is right on mixed questions. It combines signals that are each weaker alone: it learns, for example, that a crop answer agreeing with several samples is more reliable than either signal alone, and that a confident answer no other source proposes is suspicious. The head was trained to rank answers across questions, where question difficulty does most of the work, and never had to make this comparison.

Not comparable with report 10. Report 10 measured grouped AUROC on sample pools only (0.65 to 0.70 for the head). The sets here also contain crop and contrastive answers, many of them plainly wrong and easy to rank low, which raises every scorer's grouped AUROC. The comparison that matters is between scorers on the same sets. A high grouped AUROC also does not guarantee a right top choice: the head ranks most wrong crop answers low, but the few it rates highly are enough to pull its top choice below greedy.

The ranker usually keeps a confident greedy answer, so most of the gain sits in a minority of questions. The best gate here is the answer probability of the greedy answer, not the head: routing its lowest 30% reaches +1.05 of the full +1.07 points at 5.2 of 13.5 passes. The head gate needs about half the questions for the same share, and the pre-generation score, which sees no answer, is the weakest. This matches report 10: the answer probability is a weak detector across questions but a good signal of where alternative answers exist. With crops only and the same gate, 30% of questions give +0.98 points at 3.2 passes per question, about three times the cost of greedy decoding for almost the whole gain. That makes the step usable as an adaptive-compute layer: a cheap score decides where to spend, the correction step spends it.

13. Second round: grounded crops, step-by-step reasoning, and the model as its own judge

Sections 6 to 12 left three gaps: candidates came from fixed corner crops that often miss the evidence, nothing fixed ChartQA, where errors are arithmetic over values the model reads correctly, and the ranker's strongest within-question signal was still the head. The second round adds one answer to each gap and a bigger training set, all evaluated like the first round: chosen on validation, test read once.

  • Grounded crop. The model is asked for the bounding box of the region that answers the question (Qwen3-VL returns boxes on a 0–1000 grid). That region, widened by 15% on each side and at least 20% of the image, is cropped and upscaled 2×, and the question is asked on it alone (grounded crop) and as a second image next to the full image (grounded crop next to the full image). Unlike the corner crops, the crop follows the evidence.
  • Step-by-step reasoning. The model is asked to think in at most three short steps and end with "Answer: …" (256-token limit); the text after the last "Answer:" is the candidate. Replies that never reach an answer line are dropped (3% of test questions) rather than turned into a fragment. Report 08's "deeper pass" failed mostly on format; this version extracts the answer and keeps the original answer as the default unless the ranker prefers the new one.
  • The model as its own judge. For every distinct candidate, the model sees the image, the question and the candidate and is asked whether the candidate is correct; the feature is P(Yes) / (P(Yes) + P(No)) of its next token, one forward pass per candidate. It is added to the ranker's features as the probability and its logit.
  • More training. 12,000 training questions (3,000 per dataset, spread over each split) get every source, first-round and second-round, against 3,200 with first-round corrections before. Ranker v2 is trained on these only, so every training set can contain every source, in three variants: answer states and the first ranker's scalars (no judge), plus the judge (+ judge), and scalars with the judge but no states (scalars + judge).

14. What it means

  • Correction works once the choice is made by a model trained for it. The same candidates that gained 0.4 to 0.6 points under the head (and fell below greedy once crop answers were added) gain about a point under the ranker. Candidates matter second: crops are the cheapest good source, samples are a close substitute, contrastive decoding helps only on its own.
  • Detection still matters, in two places: the head score is one of the ranker's strongest features, and it is the gate that decides which questions to correct.
  • The second round shows what the remaining gains look like: a candidate source matched to a dataset's kind of error (reasoning for ChartQA's arithmetic, crops for TextVQA's small text) and a judge feature from the model itself. Combining them per question, reasoning and crops together with a ranker that knows which to trust for which question type, is the obvious next configuration.
  • The remaining headroom is large (91.99% oracle against 83.85%). The most direct ways to use it are a stronger judge (the model itself asked to compare candidates against the image, or a ranker trained with more correction candidates) and more evidence-changing candidates, not more samples.

15. Limits

  • One model size (2B). The crop grid is fixed (four corners); a question whose evidence sits in the centre is helped only by the overlap.
  • The ranker trains on correction candidates for only 3,200 of 50,517 training questions, so it saw far fewer zoom and contrastive candidates than samples.
  • The gating and per-source tables are test numbers; only the main configuration and the ranker were chosen on validation.
  • Correctness uses the thresholds of the earlier reports (VQA score ≥ 0.5, ANLS ≥ 0.9, option letter), which can count near-misses as wrong both ways.
  • Contrastive decoding uses one noise strength and one plausibility cutoff; neither was tuned.

16. Next

  1. Combine the best sources of both rounds per question (reasoning for chart and science questions, crops for text questions) and train the ranker on all 50,517 training questions with every source; the 12,000-question training set of ranker v2 is still a quarter of the data.
  2. Gate the second-round step like the first (Section 11): reasoning costs one long generation, and most questions keep the greedy answer.
  3. Improve the judge: it says Yes to most fluent answers; asking it to compare two candidates, or calibrating it per dataset, should make it a stronger feature.
  4. Repeat on Qwen3-VL-4B to see whether the gains hold with a stronger base model.
