Training Correctness Heads Across Datasets

Joint training on a mixture of ChartQA, TextVQA, DocVQA and ScienceQA, sequential training that tests what a head still remembers, and a new loss that makes the score fall when the image is corrupted.

Model: Qwen3-VL-2B. Mixture: 50,517 train, 6,420 validation, 9,517 test rows. Every number is on the pooled test split or on one dataset's rows of it.

1. Summary

  • On the pooled mixture the model's own confidence has AUROC 0.719. The best head trained with the old loss reaches 0.881 (Unified, joint mode) and the best with the new loss 0.882 (Unified, joint mode).
  • Effect of the new loss on pooled AUROC. Compared with the old loss: the separate pre-generation head did not change by more than the seed spread (-0.002 AUROC); the separate verifier did not change by more than the seed spread (-0.001 AUROC); the unified head, joint mode did not change by more than the seed spread (+0.001 AUROC); the unified head, pre-generation mode did not change by more than the seed spread (-0.001 AUROC).
  • Grounding. When the image is swapped for another one, the score of a correct answer falls in 95% of cases for the model's own confidence, 93% for the unified head with the old loss, and 96% with the new loss.
  • The grounding term is free, the pair term is not. For the unified head the grounding term alone changes pooled AUROC by +0.001 and raises the share of correct answers whose score falls under a corrupted image by +0.032. The pair term alone changes AUROC by -0.011. The new loss as specified, with both terms, therefore costs accuracy that the grounding term alone does not.
  • Heads trained on one dataset after another keep what they learned. Trained through all four datasets in sequence, the unified head loses 0.011 AUROC on earlier datasets with the old loss and 0.013 with the new loss, and ends at an average AUROC of 0.865. The answer-conditioned verifier forgets the most (0.032), and a 10% replay buffer cuts that to 0.005.
  • Grounding survives sequential training, and is higher with the new loss. After the last stage, the share of correct answers whose score falls under a corrupted image, averaged over datasets, is 92% with the old loss and 95% with the new loss, for the unified head. Under both losses it barely changes between a dataset's own stage and the end.

The mixture

The mixture is the union of the four datasets' existing splits. Its train split is the four train splits pooled, and the same for validation and test, so nothing was resampled and every test question is one the per-dataset study also scored. Four experiments

  1. The model's own confidence and linear probes on the mixture. Token confidence needs no training. The linear probes are the linear-architecture heads of experiment 2.
  2. Separate and unified heads on the mixture, old loss. The pre-generation head (reads the question state), the verifier head (reads the question and answer state), and the unified head (reads both).
  3. The same three heads with the new loss.
  4. Sequential training. The mixture trains on all datasets at once, which is the easy case. The harder question is whether a head that has been trained on dataset A still scores A well after it has been trained on B and then C. Heads are trained on one dataset at a time, in several orders, and scored on every dataset after every stage.

The new loss

P_theta(S_confidence | h_answer, h_question)         the head, trained with cross-entropy
L_contrastive(h_predicted, h_correct)                 reference answer scores above a wrong prediction
L_contrastive(h_clean, h_corrupt) -> S_confidence     score falls when the image is corrupted

The first two parts are in the old loss already (the second is the pairwise reference-answer term). The third is new: it asks that the same question and the same answer score lower when the image no longer supports the answer. In code it is softplus(S_corrupt - S_clean) on the examples the model answered correctly, averaged over the head's modes. A wrong answer has no grounding to lose, so it is excluded.

To make it possible, Qwen3-VL was run again on every example with the image replaced by a different image from the same dataset and split, keeping the question and the generated answer text. The hidden states of those passes are the corrupted inputs. The gallery shows what the corruption looks like.

6. Sequential training: does the head remember earlier datasets?

Experiments 2 and 3 train on all datasets at once. In practice a head is often trained on one dataset, then extended to another, and the worry is that training on the later dataset overwrites what it learned from the earlier one. This section tests that directly. Each head is trained on one dataset at a time, in three orders (forward, reverse, shuffled), and after every stage it is scored on the test split of all four datasets. Everything else is fixed: the head configuration is the winner of the mixture sweep for that loss, each stage runs until its own validation stops improving, and each variant is repeated on three seeds. Two things are compared: the old loss against the new loss, and plain sequential training against sequential training with a replay buffer holding 10% of every earlier dataset's training rows.

How to read the metrics. Final average is mean AUROC over the four datasets after the last stage. Forgetting is, for each dataset trained before the last stage, the best score it had after its own stage minus its final score (negative means it improved). Backward transfer is its final score minus its score right after its own stage. Retention is its final score minus the score of a head trained on that dataset alone. All are averaged over 3 orders and 3 seeds, with the spread after the plus-minus sign.

11. What the results mean

1. One head for four kinds of input works about as well as four heads

The model's own confidence reaches pooled AUROC 0.719. A head trained on the mixture reaches 0.881 with the old loss and 0.882 with the new loss. Against heads trained on one dataset each, the shared unified head differs by ChartQA +0.003, TextVQA +0.007, DocVQA +0.005, ScienceQA -0.015 AUROC. It is at least as good on ChartQA, TextVQA, DocVQA and a little worse on ScienceQA. The differences on three of the datasets are a few thousandths, so the fair reading is that the shared head matches the specialists there and is a little worse on the dataset whose questions carry the most signal on their own. A plausible reason, not tested here, is that the pool teaches the head about harder questions from the other datasets while diluting what is specific to ScienceQA. The shared head does not need to be tuned per dataset.

2. Training on later datasets barely erases earlier ones

When the heads are trained on one dataset after another, the AUROC lost on earlier datasets is small for every head: 0.013 for the pre-generation head, 0.032 for the verifier and 0.011 for the unified head, trained plainly with the old loss. After all four stages the unified head's average AUROC is 0.865, against 0.870 for heads trained on each dataset alone and 0.875 for the head trained on all of them at once. Sequential training gives up about 0.010 AUROC compared with joint training. The frozen hidden states are shared across the datasets, so most of what a head learns, which questions the model finds hard, plausibly carries over. A pre-generation head trained on ChartQA alone already scores 0.773 on TextVQA, 0.750 on DocVQA, 0.873 on ScienceQA before it has seen any of them. The verifier, which reads the answer, transfers much less (0.738, 0.703, 0.648), likely because answers look different from dataset to dataset.

3. Heads that read the answer forget more, and a small replay buffer fixes it

The verifier forgets the most (0.032), the unified head the least (0.011). With a replay buffer of 10% of every earlier dataset's training rows the verifier's forgetting drops to 0.005, and its final average rises to 0.856 from 0.834. For the heads that already forget little, replay changes nothing measurable. So the cost of forgetting sits mostly in the answer-conditioned state. A likely reason is that its form depends on the dataset (a number, a string from a page, a letter), but that was not tested separately.

4. The new loss does not change what is remembered, but it changes what the score means

For ranking quality, the new loss behaves like the old one in the sequential runs: forgetting is 0.020, 0.034 and 0.013 for the three heads, against 0.013, 0.032 and 0.011 with the old loss. What it changes is grounding. Sensitivity to the image does not fade during later training under either loss: for each dataset, the share of correct answers whose score falls under a corrupted image is within about 0.03 right after its own stage and after the last one. The difference is the level. After the last stage, averaged over datasets, that share is 92% with the old loss and 95% with the new loss for the unified head, and 86% against 91% for the pre-generation head. The gap is widest on ScienceQA, where the old-loss heads respond to the image least: many of its questions can be answered from the question text, and the old-loss head learns to lean on that. The new loss pushes the score to depend on the image even there.

5. The grounding term is free in accuracy, the pair term is not

The ablation separates the two contrastive terms. For the unified head, the grounding term alone changes pooled AUROC by +0.001 and raises grounding order accuracy by +0.032. The pair term alone (predicted answer against correct answer) changes AUROC by -0.011, and the two together by -0.009. The separate verifier shows the same pattern. The pair term was already found to hurt in the per-dataset study, and it hurts here too, so the useful part of the proposed loss is the clean-versus-corrupted term. A plausible reason, not tested here, is that the correct-answer state differs from a generated-answer state in ways that have nothing to do with correctness, so the head can reduce the pair loss by learning that difference.

6. The model's own confidence is sensitive to the image, but cannot rank answers

One result is easy to miss. When the image is swapped, the model's own answer confidence falls for 95% of correct answers. That is more than the unified head with the old loss (93%), about the same as the verifier with the old loss (95%), and less than the verifier with the new loss (99%). So the model's confidence does register that the evidence is gone. Its weakness is elsewhere: among answers on clean images it is saturated near 1.0 and ranks right against wrong poorly (pooled AUROC 0.719). The learned heads add the ranking. The old loss gives them no particular sensitivity to the image, and the new loss raises it, for the pre-generation head from 88% to 92% and for the verifier above the level of the model's own confidence.

7. What this means for the project

A correctness gate can be trained on whatever data is available, in any order, without a large penalty on what it learned earlier, and extended to a new dataset with a small replay buffer if the head reads the answer. The loss term that checks grounding can be added at no accuracy cost and gives a score that responds to the image. Whether that sensitivity makes the gate better at deciding when a correction will help is the next question, and it needs the correction step.

12. Limits

  • One model size. Only Qwen3-VL-2B was run.
  • One kind of corruption. Grounding is tested by swapping in another image from the same dataset and split. A different corruption (patch shuffle, blur) could behave differently, and the swap is only one way to remove evidence.
  • The corrupted answer is the original answer. The score is asked about the same answer text under a corrupted image. That tests whether the score notices missing support, not whether the model would have answered differently.
  • The pooled mixture is dominated by ChartQA in the training data (the largest split by far), which can favor it in the pooled numbers. Per-dataset rows are shown for that reason.
  • Forgetting here is the forgetting of a small head on frozen features. The language model is never updated, so there is no forgetting inside it. Datasets share the same hidden-state space, which is part of why forgetting is mild. A head trained on features from a model that is itself updated could forget more.
  • The sequential runs use fixed configurations. Each loss family uses the winner of the mixture sweep for that kind, not a configuration tuned for sequential training, and there are three dataset orders and three seeds. Only one mitigation was tried, a 10% replay buffer. Regularization-based methods were not.
  • Grounding on ScienceQA is hard to read. Many ScienceQA questions can be answered from the text of the question alone, so a score that falls little under a swapped image is not necessarily wrong there.
  • Test splits are the ones of the per-dataset study, carved from the validation split for TextVQA and DocVQA because official test answers are hidden.
  • Detection, not correction. Nothing here shows that a flagged answer can be fixed.
